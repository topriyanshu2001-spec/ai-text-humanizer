## Abstract

Two literatures carry the name microbiota–gut–brain axis, and they do not meet the same evidential standard. The older one treats gut microbes as a diffuse humoral influence on the brain, measured as community composition and correlated with behavioural or psychiatric outcomes. Its human evidence has not consolidated. Composition varies with diet, drugs and disease; antipsychotics and antidepressants inhibit gut bacteria directly, so the exposure and the treatment are entangled. Mendelian randomisation studies drawing instruments from the same 16S genome-wide data return mutually contradictory taxon lists, and meta-analyses of the same intervention report effects from large to null, with pooled estimates for faecal microbiota transplantation in depression spanning −1.21 to −0.10 with a confidence interval crossing zero. The second literature is anatomical. It names epithelial sensory cells, their stimuli, their transmitters and the vagal afferents they contact, then tests those connections by optogenetics, monosynaptic tracing and selective denervation. Neuropod cells transduce luminal sugars to vagal neurons in milliseconds through glutamate. Enterochromaffin cells couple irritants and metabolites to 5-HT3-bearing afferents, and drive visceral pain and anxiety when activated. A 2025 result joined the two accounts by showing that colonic neuropod cells detect a bacterial structural protein through Toll-like receptor 5 and signal feeding through peptide YY onto NPY2R vagal neurons. We argue that microbial signalling should be modelled as input to defined sensory channels rather than as systemic exposure to a community, and set out what that changes for design, endpoints and targets.

**Keywords:** microbiota–gut–brain axis; neuropod cells; vagal afferents; enterochromaffin cells; Toll-like receptor 5; causal inference; faecal microbiota transplantation

## 1. Introduction

Ask what the gut microbiota does to the brain and you get two kinds of answer.

One describes a community: certain taxa are depleted in depression, metabolites cross into circulation, germ-free animals behave strangely, and restoring the community restores the phenotype [45,46,49]. Another describes a wire. Here a specific epithelial cell detects a specific molecule and releases a transmitter onto a named vagal afferent; the signal reaches the brainstem in milliseconds [62].

Both accounts appear in the same reviews, often in adjacent paragraphs, as though they were two descriptions of one mechanism at different resolutions. They are not. Different claims, different methods — and over the past decade, evidence of markedly different quality.

Of the two, the compositional account has the larger literature and the weaker human record. Its foundational animal work is real: germ-free mice mount exaggerated corticosterone responses to restraint stress, an effect reversed by monocolonisation with *Bifidobacterium infantis*, with reduced hippocampal and cortical BDNF alongside [1]. Colonisation shapes brain development and adult behaviour [2], and microbial depletion in adolescence leaves lasting neurochemical marks [5]. Human population work has found reproducible correlations, including depletion of *Coprococcus* and *Dialister* in people reporting depression across a cohort of over a thousand with independent validation [14]. What has not followed is a causal account that survives the usual tests.

Its counterpart has the smaller literature and the stronger evidence. Bohórquez and colleagues first described the neuropod, a cytoplasmic process by which enteroendocrine cells contact neurons [61]. Kaelberer and colleagues then showed those cells form genuine synapses with vagal nodose neurons, transducing luminal sugar through glutamate on a millisecond timescale, with the connection mapped by monosynaptic rabies tracing and confirmed optogenetically [62]. Bellono and colleagues established that enterochromaffin cells are excitable chemosensors coupling irritants and metabolites to 5-HT3-bearing afferent fibres [65]. Bai and colleagues produced a genetic map of the vagal sensory neurons innervating the gut, resolving mechanoreceptive and chemoreceptive populations by marker gene [74], and Kupari and colleagues resolved 24 molecular subtypes across the vagal ganglia [75].

An anatomical fact frames the whole dispute. Between 75 and 90 per cent of vagal fibres are afferent [72,78]. What the compositional literature treats mainly as an efferent modulator of gut function is, by fibre count, overwhelmingly a sensory cable running the other way. Gut speaks to brain far more than brain speaks to gut, and the hardware for that conversation is sensory.

Until recently these two accounts had little to say to each other, because the circuit work studied nutrients and the compositional work studied microbes. In 2025 that changed. Liu and colleagues reported that colonic neuropod cells detect flagellin, a bacterial structural protein, through Toll-like receptor 5, and signal through peptide YY onto NPY2R-expressing vagal neurons to suppress feeding [64]. Microbial material is sensed by a named cell, through a named receptor, along a named line.

Our argument is that the second architecture should now organise the field, and that treating microbial influence as systemic exposure to a community has cost more than it has gained. We are not claiming the compositional account is false. We are claiming it is under-specified in a way that has made it hard to falsify, and that the specificity required to fix that is now available.

The stakes are not academic. Depressive and anxiety disorders rose sharply during the pandemic years, with an estimated 53.2 million additional cases of major depressive disorder worldwide in 2020 alone [102], and depressive disorders remain among the leading causes of years lived with disability [103]. Appetite for a tractable biological handle on them is correspondingly large. A field that offers one without being able to defend it does damage of its own.

## 2. Materials and methods

This is a critical narrative review rather than a systematic one. We registered no protocol and make no claim to exhaustive retrieval; the synthesis is interpretive, and we have tried to mark where evidence is thin.

We searched PubMed/MEDLINE, Scopus and Google Scholar across the circuit literature (*neuropod*, *enteroendocrine*, *enterochromaffin*, *vagal afferent*, *nodose*, *gut-brain circuit*), the compositional literature (*microbiota-gut-brain*, *psychobiotic*, *germ-free*, *faecal microbiota transplantation*) and the causal-inference literature applied to both (*Mendelian randomization*, *gut microbiome GWAS*). Reference lists of retrieved reviews were hand-searched. We gave priority to primary reports over commentary.

## 3. The compositional account and the state of its evidence

### 3.1 What the account claims

Stated carefully, the compositional model holds that the aggregate composition of the gut microbial community influences central nervous function through humoral routes: microbial metabolites entering portal and systemic circulation, immune mediators raised by microbial signals, and modulation of enteric and vagal tone. Cryan and colleagues set out the full version across more than a hundred pages, and it remains the field's reference statement [45]; earlier syntheses by Collins and by Foster established its shape [46,47], and shorter overviews have since consolidated it for clinical audiences [48].

Nothing in that model is implausible, and several of its proposed mediators are well evidenced. Short-chain fatty acids reach the circulation and have defined central actions [54,55]. Microbial regulation of tryptophan metabolism alters serotonergic substrate availability [56,57]. Spore-forming bacteria promote serotonin biosynthesis from colonic enterochromaffin cells, which links community composition to a transmitter the brain uses [53]. Microglial maturation depends on the microbiota, with short-chain fatty acids implicated [52]. Immune mediators do real work, and neuroimmune interaction in the gut wall is now mapped in some detail [59,60]. Epithelial barrier function modifies what reaches the circulation at all [58].

Difficulty arrives at the evidential level, not the theoretical one. Each mediator is plausible; none has been shown to carry a specified signal, from a specified microbial source, to a specified central consequence in humans.

### 3.2 Germ-free animals, and what they can establish

Germ-free rearing produces large behavioural and neuroendocrine phenotypes [1,2,3], including altered central BDNF levels that normalise on colonisation [4], and the model has carried much of the field's causal weight. Transferring microbiota from young donors into aged recipients even attenuates selected age-associated cognitive deficits [6]. Limits of that inference deserve stating plainly. Germ-free animals differ from conventional ones in gut morphology, immune development, bile acid pools, vitamin status and enteric neuronal density, so a behavioural difference localises the cause to "having been raised without microbes" and no further. Reconstitution experiments narrow this usefully, and a monocolonisation that reverses a phenotype [1] is a genuine causal result about that organism under those conditions.

What germ-free work cannot do is identify the route. An effect reversed by *B. infantis* might travel by metabolite, by immune mediator, by vagal afferent, or by altered gut transit changing something else entirely. No part of the design discriminates between these, and for two decades the field largely did not ask it to.

Behavioural findings in these animals replicate unevenly. Germ-free mice show reduced anxiety-like behaviour alongside altered central neurochemistry [3], which sits awkwardly beside the exaggerated stress-hormone response [1], since one would naively expect a more stress-reactive animal to appear more anxious rather than less. Both results are reconcilable, but reconciling them requires assumptions about which assay measures what, and assays of rodent anxiety are notoriously sensitive to handling, housing, lighting and time of day.

There is also a selection effect inside the model itself. Germ-free colonies are maintained in a small number of facilities, each with its own gnotobiotic husbandry, diet and background strain, and microbiota differences between vivaria are a recognised source of irreproducibility [7]. Phenotypes robust in one facility and absent in another get reported as failures to replicate. They may instead be true results about two different animals.

Transfer experiments appear to solve the directionality problem and partly do. Colonising germ-free mice with microbiota from patients with depression [8], schizophrenia [13] or autism [11] produces corresponding behavioural phenotypes; transfer from depressed donors into antibiotic-depleted rats induces anhedonia [9]; and maternal-immune-activation offspring improve on bacterial treatment [10]. These are striking results. They are also the ones most exposed to the critique that a 2025 *Neuron* analysis levelled at the autism literature specifically: small samples, high-dimensional exposures, no pre-specified hypothesis about which aspect of the microbiome should matter, and behavioural assays in mice whose relevance to the human condition is assumed rather than demonstrated [34].

We would extend one point from that analysis beyond autism. When a transfer experiment moves an entire community and reports a behavioural difference, the result identifies no component, and the number of candidate mediators is very large. That is a demonstration that something transferable matters, not a mechanism.

### 3.3 Human association studies

Human cohort work has produced the field's most replicable compositional findings. The Flemish Gut Flora Project analysis, with independent validation, found butyrate-producing *Faecalibacterium* and *Coprococcus* associated with higher quality-of-life indicators, and *Coprococcus* and *Dialister* depleted in depression [14]. Meta-analysis across psychiatric diagnoses reports depletion of anti-inflammatory butyrate producers and enrichment of pro-inflammatory taxa [15]. Infant gut composition has been associated with later cognitive scores [17].

Sobering, too, is the most careful synthesis available. McGuinness and colleagues reviewed 44 observational studies (2,510 cases; 2,407 controls) across major depressive disorder, bipolar disorder and schizophrenia, and found no strong evidence for consistent differences in alpha diversity, with reports of altered beta diversity that did not converge on particular taxa [16]. A decade of case–control work has established that psychiatric populations differ somewhere in community structure, and has not established where.

Why should that surprise anyone? Microbiome data are compositional, so abundances are not independent, and naive analysis generates spurious associations unless the constraint is handled explicitly [31]. Studies in this area are also small for the dimensionality involved — a combination that inflates published effect sizes and lowers the chance that any significant finding is true [32,33].

### 3.4 Confounding, and why it is unusually severe here

Diet, medication and disease state all shape the microbiome and all associate with psychiatric outcomes. Population surveys found 69 covariates associated with compositional variation in one cohort, replicating at 92 per cent [18], and 126 exogenous and intrinsic factors in another, including 19 drug groups and 60 dietary factors [19]. Daily sampling shows composition tracking food choices over days, personalised to the individual [20]. Exercise shifts composition and short-chain fatty acid production, and the shift reverses when training stops [106].

Medication deserves separate emphasis; here confounding turns near-circular. Maier and colleagues screened more than 1,000 marketed drugs against 40 gut bacterial strains and found that 24 per cent of those with human targets inhibited the growth of at least one strain, with antipsychotics overrepresented [21]. Vich Vila and colleagues found proton-pump inhibitors, metformin, antibiotics and laxatives associated with the strongest compositional signatures in population and disease cohorts [22]. Lukić and colleagues treated mice with five antidepressants and found all five reduced microbial richness, with *Ruminococcus flavefaciens* among the taxa most reduced [23]. Gut bacteria in turn metabolise a wide range of oral drugs, so the relationship runs both ways [24].

Put those together and the standard design collapses. Compare the microbiome of treated depressed patients against healthy controls, and you are comparing people who differ in diet, sleep, activity, illness — and in direct pharmacological suppression of gut bacteria. An association recovered from that comparison is compatible with the brain acting on the gut, with the drug acting on the gut, or with the gut acting on the brain, and the phrase "gut–brain axis" quietly favours the third.

### 3.5 Mendelian randomisation and what it has produced

Genetic instrumental-variable analysis ought to resolve direction, and the field has turned to it heavily. Results have not converged.

Studies published within the same two-year window report mutually inconsistent taxon lists. One identifies five taxa positively and six negatively associated with depression; another finds ten protective and ten deleterious; a third reports fifteen positive and sixteen negative causal effects, with thirty-six reverse-direction effects alongside. These are not analyses of different exposures. They are largely analyses of the same summary statistics.

Why? Look at where the instruments come from. MiBioGen, the consortium meta-analysis supplying genetic instruments for most of this work, assembled genome-wide genotypes with 16S faecal microbiome data from 18,340 individuals across 24 cohorts [25]. That is a substantial undertaking and a modest GWAS for the purpose. Host genetic effects on composition are real but few and small: the Dutch Microbiome Project found two study-wide significant signals, at *LCT* and *ABO*, in 7,738 participants [26], and a German study identified ABO histo-blood group influence among 38 suggestive loci in 8,956 individuals [27].

With few loci reaching genome-wide significance, investigators relax the inclusion threshold, commonly to 1×10⁻⁵, to obtain enough instruments to run the analysis. Consequences here deserve stating precisely, since they are often described loosely. In two-sample Mendelian randomisation without sample overlap, weak instruments bias estimates toward the null rather than toward the confounded observational association [28]. Weak instruments therefore do not, by themselves, manufacture the positive findings this literature reports.

What does is the combination of a relaxed threshold with multiplicity and analytic flexibility. Run hundreds of taxa against dozens of psychiatric outcomes, with unregistered analytic choices and inconsistent sensitivity testing, and contradictory significant findings are the expected output [29]. Relaxed thresholds also admit variants more likely to violate the exclusion restriction through pleiotropy, which existing sensitivity methods detect imperfectly [30]. This pattern is a feature of the method as applied, not evidence about biology.

### 3.6 Intervention trials

Randomised interventions should settle matters, and the meta-analytic record is where the compositional account is most exposed.

Individual trials do report benefit. A *Lactobacillus helveticus* and *Bifidobacterium longum* formulation reduced psychological distress in volunteers [35]; a multispecies preparation reduced cognitive reactivity to sad mood in healthy participants [36]; a probiotic and prebiotic combination improved psychological outcomes in major depressive disorder [37]. Dietary interventions, which act on the microbiome among much else, improved depressive symptoms in the SMILES trial [43] and in meta-analysis [44].

Pooled estimates for probiotics are large and unstable. One synthesis across eighteen trials in 969 participants reported a standardised mean difference of −0.96 (95% CI −1.31 to −0.61) with between-study heterogeneity at I² = 85%, and the pooled effect fell by roughly a third once high risk-of-bias studies were excluded [38]. Another reported −0.53 (95% CI −0.67 to −0.39) [38].

Faecal microbiota transplantation shows the problem in sharper form. Early case reports and open-label series were encouraging [40,41], and a pilot randomised trial established feasibility and safety [39]. One meta-analysis reports a standardised mean difference near −1.21 for depressive symptoms, another a Hedges' g of −0.81, and a third, pooling seven studies in 235 participants, reports −0.10 with a confidence interval from −0.60 to 0.41 [42]. One intervention, one outcome, conclusions running from large benefit to nothing.

Heterogeneity at I² = 85% is not a nuisance parameter to be noted and moved past. Such a value says the trials are not estimating a common effect — what one expects when "probiotic" names hundreds of distinct organisms at different doses in different hosts, and when the mechanism specifying which organism should help which patient has never been identified [50,51].

Imagine pooling every monoamine-targeting drug ever tested, across every dose and every psychiatric indication, under the heading "antidepressant". Reviewers would reject it as incoherent before anyone examined the forest plot. Pooling *Lactobacillus* and *Bifidobacterium* preparations at doses spanning orders of magnitude, in hosts whose resident communities differ more than their genomes do, is the same error wearing different clothes.

Blinding deserves separate mention. Probiotic and transplantation trials frequently produce gastrointestinal symptoms that are themselves perceptible, and depression endpoints are self-reported. Unblinding by side effect inflates effects on subjective outcomes, which is one reason the drop in pooled effect after excluding high risk-of-bias studies is informative rather than incidental [38].

None of this establishes that the interventions do nothing. Some of these trials report real benefit, and a mechanism may well exist. Our point is narrower: the literature as it stands cannot distinguish a true modest effect in a responsive subgroup from a bias-driven effect in a heterogeneous mixture, and no further pooling of the same trials will separate them.

## 4. The circuit account

### 4.1 Neuropod cells and synaptic transduction

Enteroendocrine cells were long modelled as endocrine sensors releasing peptides into circulation, acting over minutes, and that role is real and well characterised [69]. Bohórquez and colleagues then described a cytoplasmic process, the neuropod, by which these cells contact neurons directly [61]. Kaelberer and colleagues showed that a subset forms true synapses with vagal nodose neurons [62]. Optogenetic activation elicited excitatory postsynaptic potentials in connected nodose neurons within milliseconds, glutamate carried the fast transmission, and monosynaptic rabies tracing [88] established the connection anatomically. These cells place the intestinal lumen one synapse from the brainstem.

Timescale is the conceptual break. No humoral signal delivers stimulus-specific information in milliseconds; and between a hormone diffusing to a receptor field and a synapse addressing a named postsynaptic partner lies the distinction between broadcast and addressed transmission.

That distinction does real work. Broadcast signalling carries magnitude: more ligand, more effect, with specificity supplied by which tissues express the receptor. Addressed signalling carries identity as well, because the postsynaptic partner is determined by anatomy rather than by receptor distribution, so two stimuli can produce different central consequences even when they release the same transmitter from neighbouring cells. Any field committed to measuring circulating metabolite concentrations implicitly assumes the first model. Where the second operates, the informative variable is not how much of a molecule is present but which line it activated.

Buchanan and colleagues demonstrated exactly that dissociation. Duodenal neuropod cells transduce sugars and non-caloric sweeteners alike to the vagus, using sweet taste receptors and sodium–glucose transporters [63], machinery first identified in the gut by Margolskee and colleagues [70,71]. The two stimuli recruit different transmitters: sweetener evokes purinergic signalling, sugar evokes glutamatergic signalling, and the animal's preference for sugar depends on the glutamatergic arm [63]. Identical sweetness, identical cell type, different line, different behaviour. A concentration measurement could not have distinguished them.

### 4.2 Enterochromaffin chemosensation

Enterochromaffin cells make up under one per cent of intestinal epithelium and produce the large majority of the body's serotonin. Bellono and colleagues established that they are electrically excitable, express voltage-gated channels, detect irritants, metabolites and catecholamines through defined receptors, and release serotonin through P/Q-type calcium channels onto 5-HT3-expressing afferent fibres in synaptic-like contacts [65]. Alcaino and colleagues showed a subpopulation is mechanosensitive and requires Piezo2 to convert force into serotonin release [66].

That result matters for the compositional account specifically. Microbial metabolites were previously assumed to act after absorption, at distant targets; here they act at the epithelial surface, on a sensory cell, and the information travels neurally. Microbial regulation of enterochromaffin serotonin synthesis [53] therefore has a defined local consequence rather than only a systemic one.

Bayrer and colleagues then closed an important gap in that story. Selective activation of enterochromaffin cells increased serotonin release, produced visceral hypersensitivity, and generated anxiety-like behaviour [67]. An identified epithelial sensory cell, manipulated directly, changes an affective readout. No longer is the circuit literature confined to feeding.

### 4.3 The vagal sensory map

Anatomists characterised the afferent vagus well before genetic tools arrived, describing distinct terminal structures across organs [72]. Williams and colleagues then used genetic access to separate two functional classes: GPR65 neurons innervating intestinal villi and responding to nutrients, and GLP1R neurons innervating gut muscle and responding to stretch [73]. Bai and colleagues applied target-specific single-cell sequencing to the vagal neurons innervating the gastrointestinal tract and resolved populations by marker gene, identifying GLP1R-expressing intraganglionic laminar endings in the stomach and Piezo2-expressing gastric distension neurons regulating satiety [74]. Kupari and colleagues produced an atlas resolving 24 vagal subtypes [75]. Spinal afferents have been resolved in parallel, with seven transcriptional classes of colonic sensory neuron described [76].

So the vagus is not a trunk line carrying undifferentiated visceral tone. Think of it instead as a set of labelled lines, each with its own peripheral terminals, stimuli and central targets; individual lines can be manipulated genetically [89].

Interoceptive signalling, long described in psychological and neuroanatomical terms [90,91], now has a cellular substrate at the gut end of the pathway, and vagal routes have been proposed as modulators of psychiatric and inflammatory disease [79]. Central organisation follows that same logic. Huang and colleagues found that hindbrain GLP1R populations mediating satiety and aversion are functionally separable, with NTS-GLP1R activation producing satiety without aversion and AP-GLP1R activation producing aversion [82]. Vagal GLP1R signalling is required for normal food intake and glycaemia [80], and the therapeutic exploitation of this axis is now the largest clinical application of gut–brain biology in existence [81]. None of that progress came from measuring community composition.

### 4.4 The neurobiotic sense

Liu and colleagues reported in 2025 that flagellin, the structural protein of the bacterial flagellum, stimulates Toll-like receptor 5 on peptide YY-labelled colonic neuropod cells, which release PYY onto NPY2R-expressing vagal nodose neurons and so regulate feeding [64]. Liu and colleagues call this a neurobiotic sense: the host monitoring a microbial molecular pattern and adjusting behaviour accordingly.

Consider how much more constrained this claim is than a compositional one. Named here are the ligand, the receptor, the cell, the transmitter, the postsynaptic receptor, the afferent population and the behavioural output. Every element can be deleted or stimulated independently. Claims of that shape can be wrong in specific ways, which is what makes them worth testing.

### 4.5 Beyond the vagus

Vagal afferents are not the only route, and the circuit literature has begun to map others. Muller and colleagues identified a gut–brain circuit by which microbiota modulate sympathetic neurons, using gnotobiotic models, circuit tracing and chemogenetics together [77]. With roughly 500 million neurons, the enteric nervous system performs substantial sensory and motor computation of its own [83,84], and enteric glia participate in signalling rather than merely supporting it [86]. Enteric circuits are implicated in several neurological disorders [85], and inflammation leaves lasting plastic changes in gut sensory function [87].

## 5. Why the two literatures differ in strength

This asymmetry is not about effort or funding, nor is one field more rigorous by temperament. It follows from what each account specifies.

Circuit claims name their parts. Naming parts generates interventions — delete the receptor, silence the cell, cut the afferent — and the prediction either holds or fails. Compositional claims name a community state and an outcome, leaving the connecting path unspecified, so the available test is to perturb the community and observe the outcome. That test is weak because the perturbation is poorly characterised, the mediating path is unknown, and the outcome is a syndrome rather than a physiological variable.

Measurement resolution compounds it. 16S profiling resolves genus; the functional unit may be a strain, a gene cluster, or a single molecule such as flagellin [64]. Taxa are an imperfect proxy for the thing that signals, so even an accurately measured community may be the wrong variable, and the compositional constraint on the data adds a further analytic hazard [31].

### 5.1 A precedent from sensory neuroscience

Olfaction went through this transition, and the shape of it is instructive.

For most of the twentieth century, smell was studied as a chemical sense. Investigators catalogued perceived odour classes, proposed stereochemical theories of how molecular shape produced sensation, and measured detection thresholds. Careful work, and no consolidation, because the connecting path between a molecule in the air and a percept in the brain was unspecified.

Buck and Axel broke it open by finding the receptors, identifying a large multigene family of olfactory receptor genes and establishing the molecular basis of odour recognition [100]. What followed was not a refinement of odour-class taxonomy but a different discipline: receptor deorphanisation, glomerular mapping [101], labelled-line tracing from epithelium to cortex. The Nobel committee recognised it in 2004.

Microbiota–gut–brain research, we contend, currently sits where olfaction sat before 1991. There is a real phenomenon, a large descriptive literature organised around the stimulus side, and no molecular account of transduction. Flagellin binding TLR5 on a PYY-labelled colonic cell [64] is the first entry in the gut's version of that receptor catalogue, and the human enteroendocrine sensor screen now under way [68] is the beginning of the deorphanisation phase.

That analogy carries a warning as well. Receptor identification did not make the perceptual literature worthless, nor did it immediately explain why particular odours are pleasant. What changed was the standard of explanation, and a class of question that had been argued about became answerable.

## 6. What the reframing changes

### 6.1 Experimental design

Treating microbial signalling as sensory input changes the controls an experiment needs. If flagellin acts through TLR5 on colonic neuropod cells [64], then an experiment colonising mice with a flagellated organism must include a flagellin-deficient mutant and a TLR5-deficient host, because otherwise the result is attributed to a taxon when it belongs to a molecule and a receptor.

Vagotomy controls become informative rather than decorative, and the available denervation is now selective. Where whole-trunk vagotomy removes all afferent and efferent traffic, genetic access to marked vagal populations [74,75] permits silencing one line while leaving the rest intact, using tools developed for exactly this purpose [89].

### 6.2 Endpoints and biomarkers

Composition is a poor biomarker for a signalling event, because it reports standing community structure rather than whether a channel fired. Where the ligand is known, the ligand can be measured. Luminal and mucosal flagellin concentration is a more direct correlate of TLR5-mediated signalling than the relative abundance of flagellated taxa, and the two can dissociate.

Stratifying trial participants by taxon abundance will misclassify anyone whose community carries the taxon without presenting the ligand at the epithelial surface. Given how poorly taxa have performed as predictors across the trial literature [38,42], that misclassification is a live candidate explanation rather than a hypothetical one.

### 6.3 Therapeutic targets

Psychobiotic development has proceeded largely by screening organisms against behavioural endpoints [50,51], which is a reasonable strategy when no mechanism is known and an inefficient one afterwards. Channel-level accounts offer defined targets: the receptor, the transmitter release step, the postsynaptic receptor, the afferent population.

Whether those targets prove tractable is open. We would note that the pharmacology already exists in adjacent form, since NPY2R and 5-HT3 are established drug targets already, and that gut-restricted compounds avoid much of the central exposure that complicates neuropsychiatric drug development. The GLP-1 field is the proof of concept: dissecting which hindbrain population mediates satiety and which mediates aversion [82] opens a path to separating efficacy from the side effect that limits adherence, and no amount of community profiling would have identified it.

A second therapeutic consequence concerns live organisms. If a defined microbial molecule activates a defined receptor, then engineering an organism to present or withhold that molecule becomes a rational design problem rather than a screening exercise, and the relevant property of a candidate strain is what ligands it displays rather than where it sits on a phylogenetic tree. Two strains of the same species can differ in flagellin expression; one would be expected to engage the channel and the other not.

This logic cuts against some current practice. Selecting probiotic candidates by genus (still common) means selecting on a variable that may not track the signalling property at all.

| Research decision | Under the compositional account | Under a sensory-channel account |
|---|---|---|
| Choosing an exposure | A taxon, a consortium, or a whole community transfer | A defined microbial molecule, with presence and absence controls |
| Required controls | Vehicle, sometimes heat-killed organism | Ligand-deficient mutant organism; receptor-deficient host; selective denervation [89] |
| Primary measurement | Community composition by sequencing | Ligand concentration at the mucosal surface; channel activation |
| Denervation control | Whole-trunk vagotomy, if used at all | Genetically targeted silencing of a marked afferent population [74,75] |
| Patient stratification | Taxon abundance | Ligand presentation and receptor genotype |
| Candidate therapeutic | Organism screened against a behavioural endpoint [50,51] | Receptor, transmitter release step, or afferent population [82] |

## 7. Where the accounts meet: α-synuclein and the vagus

Parkinson's disease supplies the clearest case of a microbial-adjacent hypothesis tested at circuit resolution.

Braak and colleagues proposed that sporadic Parkinson's pathology begins outside the midbrain and ascends, with the lower brainstem and olfactory structures affected early [92], a staging scheme later developed into the brain-first and body-first subtype hypothesis [93]. Compositional work established that gut microbiota differ in Parkinson's and correlate with clinical phenotype [99], and that microbiota are required for motor deficits and neuroinflammation in an α-synuclein-overexpressing model [12].

Circuit work then tested the route directly. Kim and colleagues injected pathological α-synuclein into the mouse stomach and observed transneuronal spread to the brain with dopaminergic loss and behavioural deficits [94]. Truncal vagotomy prevented it, as did α-synuclein deficiency. Challis and colleagues found the same gut-seeded spread occurs in aged but not young mice, which adds a biological variable the compositional framing does not supply [95].

Human evidence runs weaker, and contested. A Danish registry analysis found reduced subsequent Parkinson's risk after full truncal vagotomy [96]; a contemporaneous analysis found no association [97]; a Swedish register-based matched-cohort study comparing 9,430 vagotomy patients against 377,200 controls found 1.07 per cent versus 1.28 per cent subsequent diagnoses, a difference too small to settle the question [98]. Such disagreement is instructive rather than disqualifying: registry studies of historical surgical cohorts carry confounding by indication, and incomplete outcome capture besides.

Notice also what the α-synuclein work did *not* require. No microbiome sequencing, no taxon list, no community-level exposure. Their hypothesis concerned a protein and a nerve, and was tested on those terms. Microbial involvement in Parkinson's may well be real, and if it is, the route identified here is where a microbial trigger would have to act. Composition would then matter as an upstream determinant of whether the triggering molecule is present, which is a sharper and more testable role than the one the compositional literature usually assigns it.

## 8. Limits of the circuit account

Fairness requires stating what the circuit literature has not established, and the list is substantial.

Nearly all of it remains rodent work. Human enteroendocrine and neuropod biology is characterised far less well, vagal innervation density and subtype distribution differ between species, and the colon-to-brainstem anatomy that carries the flagellin signal in mice [64] has no verified human counterpart. The recent description and functional validation of human enteroendocrine sensors in organoids derived from duodenal biopsy [68] is the most substantial step toward closing that gap, and it is a beginning rather than a resolution.

Behavioural readouts stay narrow. Feeding, satiety and visceral sensation remain tractable, and disorders of gut–brain interaction such as irritable bowel syndrome offer a clinical domain where sensory mechanisms are already central to the disease model [104,105]; mood, cognition and social behaviour, which drive most clinical interest in this field, are reached less directly. Here we must qualify our own earlier framing: the enterochromaffin work showing that direct cellular activation produces anxiety-like behaviour alongside visceral hypersensitivity [67] demonstrates that an identified epithelial sensor can move an affective measure. That is a real advance on feeding-only readouts, and it falls well short of showing that channels explain depression. We are not claiming they do.

Neither does the circuit account subsume the humoral one. Microbial metabolites unquestionably enter circulation and act centrally [54,55], immune mediators do real work [52,59,60], tryptophan handling shifts with microbial composition [56,57], and some gut-to-brain influence is genuinely slow and diffuse. Our claim is about where specificity is currently available and therefore where causal progress is most likely, not that one channel class is exclusively real.

Selection bias in the circuit literature also deserves acknowledgement. Channels get discovered where tools exist, which currently means the vagus, and spinal afferent and enteric routes may be underrepresented for methodological rather than biological reasons [76,77]. Consider that the enteric nervous system contains more neurons than the spinal cord and operates with substantial autonomy [83,84], yet it features far less in gut-to-brain circuit work than its size would predict, largely because genetic access to its subtypes has lagged.

One harder objection deserves stating in its strongest form. One might argue that specificity is being purchased with relevance: that neuropod circuits regulate feeding over seconds, while the clinical questions concern mood over months, and that a millisecond synapse is simply the wrong timescale for depression. We take this seriously. Our answer is that sustained states are built from repeated transient signalling, that the same objection would have ruled out studying synapses in order to understand memory, and that the anxiety phenotype produced by enterochromaffin activation [67] shows affective consequences are reachable from this level. The objection is nonetheless unresolved, and it marks the largest gap between what the circuit literature has shown and what the field wants to know.

## 9. A research agenda

Four lines follow, in rough order of tractability.

Microbial molecular patterns beyond flagellin should be screened against epithelial sensory cells directly. Flagellin was a reasonable first candidate because TLR5 is well characterised; peptidoglycan fragments, lipopolysaccharide variants and quorum-sensing molecules are open, and the assay is the one already used [64]. The human enteroendocrine sensor screen provides the complementary approach from the receptor side [68].

Human tissue work remains the binding constraint. Organoid and *ex vivo* preparations from human intestinal biopsy, combined with human nodose tissue where obtainable, would establish whether the cell types and connections identified in mice exist in people [68], and that question is prior to any clinical inference.

Reanalysis of existing intervention trials by ligand rather than by taxon is cheap where samples were banked. Trials that measured community composition and found nothing may contain a signal at the molecular level, and that is a testable proposition rather than a hopeful one.

Registered, instrument-strength-reported Mendelian randomisation would improve the causal-inference literature at low cost [29,30]. We would go further: given the instrument limitations documented above [25–27], the field would lose little by declaring a moratorium on relaxed-threshold two-sample MR of 16S data until better-powered GWAS exist.

Beyond those four, one experiment would test our central contention directly. Take an intervention that works in rodents — a colonising organism that changes feeding or anxiety-like behaviour — and ask whether its effect survives selective silencing of the implicated afferent population while the organism remains present and the community unchanged. If behaviour tracks the nerve rather than the community, the channel account is doing the explanatory work. If the effect persists with the line silenced, a humoral route is carrying it, and we would be wrong in an informative way.

We would also encourage reporting that keeps the two accounts distinguishable. A paper that establishes a compositional association and then speculates about vagal mediation in its discussion, without testing it, should say so in those terms. Much of the apparent mechanistic depth in this literature lives in discussion sections rather than in results.

### 9.1 What would change clinically

Clinical translation sits further off than the enthusiasm suggests, and deserves stating concretely.

Near term, realistic gains lie in stratification rather than new therapy. If ligand presentation at the mucosal surface predicts who responds to a microbial intervention, then existing trials could be rescued by re-analysis, and future trials could enrol on a mechanistic variable instead of a diagnosis. Trials in this field have been enrolling syndromes and measuring communities, with no variable in between.

Medium term, the targets in Table 4 are approachable with conventional pharmacology, and gut-restricted agents are an established formulation strategy. The GLP-1 precedent shows what circuit-level resolution buys: separating the population that mediates benefit from the one that mediates the limiting side effect [82].

Long term, the interesting possibility turns diagnostic rather than therapeutic. A sensory channel that fires in response to a defined microbial molecule is, in principle, a readout of luminal state. Whether that readout carries clinically useful information about anything beyond feeding is unknown, and we would caution against assuming it does.

## 10. Limitations

This review is interpretive; its search was not exhaustive; and we read only English-language sources. Our reference list is weighted toward the circuit literature relative to its size in the field, which reflects our argument and should be read as a position rather than a survey. We have treated the compositional literature at the level of its meta-analytic summaries rather than evaluating individual trials, so our characterisation of its heterogeneity is coarse. The Mendelian randomisation studies we describe in Section 3.5 are characterised by their reported findings rather than by independent re-analysis, which we did not perform. Our proposal that sensory-channel specification should organise the field is an argument about research strategy, and we offer no evidence that adopting it produces better clinical outcomes, since that evidence cannot exist yet.

## 11. Conclusions

Microbiota–gut–brain research has an unusual shape for a mature discipline: thousands of papers, a reference physiology review running past two thousand pages [45], considerable clinical enthusiasm, and almost no mechanism established end-to-end in humans.

Explanation lies partly in method, partly in concept. Correlate a community-level exposure with a syndrome-level outcome, specify no path between them, and you have a claim that resists refutation; a field that cannot refute its claims cannot consolidate them either. Contradictory Mendelian randomisation results, meta-analyses disagreeing about whether faecal transplantation helps depression [42], and a systematic review finding no consistent compositional signature across 44 studies [16] are what that looks like from the outside.

Neuroepithelial work points the other way. Flagellin, TLR5, a PYY-labelled colonic cell, NPY2R vagal neurons, feeding behaviour [64]: each element is nameable, deletable and therefore falsifiable. That is a narrower claim than anything the compositional literature makes, and narrower is why it will travel further.
