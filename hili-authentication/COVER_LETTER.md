**Re: Submission of a Review article — "Causality without identity: botanical authentication as the missing axis in herb-induced liver injury assessment"**

Dear [Editor-in-Chief name],

We submit the above manuscript for consideration as a Review in the *Journal of
Ethnopharmacology*.

**What the paper says.** Hepatologists assess suspected herb-induced liver injury with
instruments built for single-molecule pharmaceuticals, chiefly RUCAM. Those instruments score
the timing of an exposure, the response to withdrawal, and the exclusion of competing causes.
Every one of them assumes the implicated agent is known. For a traditional polyherbal product
that assumption is the open question rather than a given, and the instrument has no field in
which to record that it is open. We examine the 2021–2023 *Tinospora cordifolia* controversy as
a worked example: causality was scored competently, the dispute was never about timing, and it
turned entirely on whether anyone could say what the patients had taken.

**What is new here.** Existing reviews in this area catalogue which herbs injure the liver.
None, as far as we can establish, interrogates the assessment instrument itself. Our
contribution is threefold. We show that exposure identity is an axis orthogonal to causality
and currently unrecorded. We identify, from the authentication data, that the limit is not
clinician diligence but analytical reach: when investigators in the *Tinospora* series analysed
retrieved material, all four raw plant samples were identified by morphology, microscopy, HPTLC
and DNA barcoding, while neither commercial preparation could be analysed by any method, because
co-formulated ingredients interfered. We then propose a five-level identity-certainty scale to
be reported alongside causality, with sample retention as its precondition.

The argument constrains both sides of a polarised literature, and we think that is a feature.
An unauthenticated record cannot support the claim that these herbs are unusually hepatotoxic,
and it equally cannot support the reply that adulteration explains the injuries away. Neither
camp can have what it wants from the evidence as it stands.

**Why this journal.** The *Journal of Ethnopharmacology* already requires, for primary research,
full botanical names with authorities per World Flora Online and a voucher specimen deposited in
an official herbarium. Our paper is about what happens when that standard is absent from the
clinical literature on the same plants. The asymmetry we describe — a pharmacology paper must
authenticate its material, a case report implicating the same species need not — is one this
journal is unusually well placed to address, and the readership is the one that can act on it.
We are aware of the journal's engagement with the ConPhyMP reporting standards, and our proposal
extends that logic from the bench to the clinical case record.

## Before sending

- Address a named Editor-in-Chief. "Dear Editor" costs you nothing good.
- Cite two or three recent papers from this journal that your work speaks to, in the scope
  paragraph. Fit is the thing editors triage on.
- Confirm the current word limit for Reviews at this journal. The length here (5,140 words main
  text) sits inside the usual narrative-review envelope, but verify rather than assume.
- Close out `SOURCES.md` first. Several reference fields still need confirming, and the Methods
  section still carries a placeholder for the search dates.
