[p4] For three decades the pharmacology trailed the biology. Triptans were designed as cranial
[p4] vasoconstrictors and only later understood to act partly on trigeminal neurons; the classic
[p4] preventives were borrowed from cardiology, epilepsy and psychiatry, and clinicians chose
[p4] among them largely by which adverse effect a patient could tolerate. Calcitonin gene-related
[p4] peptide (CGRP) broke that pattern. It moved from a peptide measured in cranial venous blood
[p4] to a validated target, and four monoclonal antibodies plus a family of small-molecule receptor
[p4] antagonists (the gepants) now occupy ground once held by propranolol and topiramate [4]. In
[p4] 2024 the American Headache Society went further and endorsed CGRP-targeted therapy as a
[p4] first-line preventive option [5].
[p4] So the question has moved. Nobody now asks whether CGRP matters.
[p5] Why, then, does blocking it leave so many patients where they started? In STRIVE, the
[p5] erenumab registration trial, half of those on the higher dose failed to halve their monthly
[p5] migraine days; among patients who had already failed two to four older preventives only 30%
[p5] responded [6,7]. Something other than CGRP drives their attacks. Candidates exist (pituitary
[p5] adenylate cyclase-activating polypeptide, ATP-sensitive potassium channels, amylin
[p5] signalling, hypothalamic state change), but they are only now reaching human testing [8–10].
[p5] Ayurveda enters the argument here, and it enters carrying a problem. Classical texts describe
[p5] Ardhavabhedaka, a periodic, one-sided head pain that maps closely onto migraine, together
[p5] with nasal, dietary, herbal and procedural treatments [11,12]. Indian patients use these
[p5] treatments widely, frequently alongside conventional drugs and seldom with their neurologist's
[p5] knowledge. What supports them, though, is a scatter of small trials and case series, padded out
[p5] with single-compound pharmacology and rarely built around ICHD-3 diagnostic criteria or the
[p5] outcome measures a headache specialist would accept [13,14].
[p5] This review takes a deliberately critical line. We first reconstruct migraine as a disorder of
[p5] brain state rather than of blood vessels, then examine where CGRP-era drugs succeed and
[p5] where they stall. Only then do we test Ayurvedic claims, holding them to the same mechanistic
[p5] and clinical standards. Reconciliation for its own sake is not the goal. We want to know which
[p5] Ayurvedic interventions carry enough biological plausibility and human data to justify rigorous
[p5] trials in exactly the patients modern pharmacology is failing.

[p6] The premonitory phase and the hypothalamus
[p6] Hours before the headache, sometimes a full day ahead, many patients yawn repeatedly, crave
[p6] sweet food, feel neck stiffness, tire easily or start squinting at ordinary light [15,16]. A vascular
[p6] theory cannot explain those symptoms. A hypothalamic one explains them without strain.
[p6] Clinicians tend to think in attacks; patients live in cycles. Modern descriptions divide each
[p6] episode into premonitory, aura, headache and postdrome phases, with aura present in roughly
[p6] a third of patients, and the brain between attacks is not quiet either. Neurophysiological studies
[p6] show reduced habituation to repeated sensory stimuli and altered thalamocortical processing in
[p6] the interictal period [15,16]. Migraine, on this view, is a disorder of sensory gain whose set
[p6] point drifts over days, and the attack is the moment the drift crosses a threshold. Triggers (a
[p6] skipped meal, a lost night's sleep, a fall in oestrogen) then look less like causes and more like
[p6] nudges applied to a system already close to its edge.
[p6] Direct human evidence came from an unusual experiment. Schulte and May scanned one
[p6] patient every day for 30 days. Three spontaneous attacks fell inside that window, and the scans
[p6] showed hypothalamic activity rising in the 24 h before headache onset and coupling to the
[p6] spinal trigeminal nuclei; once pain began, the coupling shifted to the dorsal rostral pons [17].

[p9] Downstream convergence on potassium channels
[p9] CGRP and PACAP each raise intracellular cyclic AMP, and much of what follows converges
[p9] on potassium channels in arterial smooth muscle. Levcromakalim, an opener of ATP-sensitive
[p9] potassium (KATP) channels, triggered migraine attacks in all 16 patients who received it in a
[p9] crossover provocation study [8]. No other human trigger comes close to that reliability.
[p9] If opening KATP channels is sufficient to start an attack, closing them should help. The
[p9] obstacle is chemistry. Glibenclamide and related sulfonylureas close KATP channels in
[p9] pancreatic β-cells long before they reach useful vascular concentrations, and selective blockers
[p9] of the vascular Kir6.1/SUR2B isoform have proven stubbornly hard to make.
[p9] Genetics supports a mixed neuronal and vascular picture. A 2022 genome-wide meta-analysis
[p9] of 102,084 cases identified 123 risk loci, with enrichment in vascular as well as central nervous
[p9] system tissue; two loci contain genes encoding targets of approved drugs, CALCA/CALCB for
[p9] CGRP and HTR1F for lasmiditan [26]. Common variants explain a small slice of heritability.
[p9] None yet predicts who will respond to which drug.

[p13] Tolerability, persistence and access
[p13] What CGRP-targeted drugs changed most was not efficacy but persistence. Older oral
[p13] preventives were abandoned at striking rates: in a United States claims analysis of chronic
[p13] migraine, only about a quarter of patients were still taking their first oral preventive six months
[p13] after starting it, and fewer than one in five remained at one year [39]. Monthly or quarterly
[p13] injections with few systemic adverse effects kept people on treatment. That tolerability profile,
[p13] more than any leap in potency, underpins the 2024 American Headache Society position that
[p13] CGRP-targeted drugs may be used first-line, without prior failure of older agents [5].

[p14] Why does blocking CGRP fail? At least five explanations compete; they overlap.
[p14] The first is simply the wrong peptide. PACAP38 provokes attacks that CGRP blockade does
[p14] not prevent [25], amylin acts through a receptor ligand antibodies leave untouched [9], and
[p14] KATP opening sits downstream of both [8]. A patient whose attacks run mainly through one
[p14] of these routes may never respond to a CGRP antibody, however long the trial of therapy.

[p20] Ginger (Shunthi): TRPV1 and a split verdict
[p20] Gingerols, the pungent principles of Zingiber officinale, are agonists at TRPV1 (formerly
[p20] VR1), with potency rising with alkyl chain length [44]. A TRPV1 agonist as a headache remedy
[p20] sounds backwards, until one recalls that sustained agonism leaves the channel refractory, the
[p20] principle behind topical capsaicin analgesia; on trigeminal C fibres, where TRPV1 and CGRP
[p20] co-localise [27], desensitisation could in principle blunt CGRP release.
[p20] Clinical data split cleanly by indication. As acute treatment, ginger powder matched
[p20] sumatriptan across five consecutive attacks in a double-blind trial of 100 patients, with fewer
[p20] adverse effects [45], and 400 mg of an extract containing 5% active ingredient, added to
[p20] ketoprofen, improved pain relief at 1, 1.5 and 2 h in 60 patients [46].
